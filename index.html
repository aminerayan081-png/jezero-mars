3. Geological Exploration

REAL DATA Four features of Jezero Crater that NASA’s Perseverance rover and orbiting spacecraft have studied. Each card explains what the feature is, what scientists found, what it tells us about ancient Mars, and why it could matter for people exploring Mars one day.

Facts come from NASA, USGS and published studies, with sources under each card. Boxes marked OUR EXPLANATION are our own reasoning from those findings, not official NASA plans. Science keeps changing, so some answers are still debated.

Feature 1 of 4 · The whole crater

The ancient lake and river

A river once flowed into Jezero and filled it with a lake. The lake has dried up.

In short

Jezero is a crater about 45 km (28 miles) wide. Long ago a river carried water, sand and mud into it, and a lake formed. The river dropped its load where it entered the lake and built a fan-shaped pile called a delta.

What scientists found

  • Rover images of layers in the delta confirmed that a river and a lake were really here. This was reported in the journal Science in 2021.
  • An ancient river valley called Neretva Vallis, about 400 m wide, was carved by water flowing into the crater.
  • Early on, the lake was high enough to spill over the eastern rim. Later it rose and fell by tens of metres and finally disappeared.
  • NASA says some landforms at Jezero date back as far as 3.6 billion years.

Why it matters for understanding ancient Mars

It shows that liquid water stayed on the surface of Mars long enough to build a delta. NASA chose Jezero in 2018 because a lake-and-delta system like this could preserve signs of past life. Whether life ever existed there is still unknown.

Why it could matter for human exploration OUR EXPLANATION

Knowing where and when water flowed helps planners decide which parts of Mars have the most interesting history, so that a crew’s limited time can be spent well. It does not mean there is usable water at Jezero today. The lake is gone, and this section has no data on water there now.

Sources: NASA/JPL, October 2021 · NASA/JPL, landing site announcement, 2018 · NASA/JPL, September 2025

Feature 2 of 4 · Western delta

The western delta and its deposits

Layers of sand, mud and boulders built up where the river met the lake.

In short

On the western side of the crater the delta is cut by long, steep slopes called scarps. They show the delta’s layers like pages in a book. Kodiak is a small hill that is a leftover piece of the delta.

What scientists found

  • The layers in Kodiak tilt in some places and lie flat in others, just like the layers in river deltas on Earth.
  • The lower parts of the scarps show similar layering. Higher up there are boulders up to about 1.5 m (5 ft) wide that came from highlands outside the crater.
  • The team concludes that powerful flash floods carried the boulders in, with water moving at roughly 6 to 30 km/h (4 to 20 mph).
  • The finest-grained mud at the bottom of the delta may hold the best traces of organic molecules and possible signs of life.

Why it matters for understanding ancient Mars

The water history was not just a slow, gentle river. It also included sudden, powerful floods, and the lake level changed over time. Fine mud settles in calm water, which is why it is such a promising place to look for preserved chemical clues.

Why it could matter for human exploration OUR EXPLANATION

Material carried in from a wide area can bring many rock types to one place, which NASA highlighted when it chose Jezero. That is valuable for explorers with limited time. The same terrain also has hazards: NASA described boulders, cliffs and wind-blown sand traps at Jezero, so steep scarps and boulder fields would need careful route planning.

A 2022 NASA planetary-protection presentation also says crewed missions cannot be fully sealed off and that crews will be exposed to Martian material, so contamination has to be managed. Places that may hold organic traces would need special care.

Sources: NASA/JPL, October 2021 · NASA/JPL, landing site announcement, 2018 · NASA planetary protection presentation, 2022 (PDF) · Image page (NASA)

Feature 3 of 4 · Crater floor

The crater floor: Máaz and Séítah

The floor is made of rock that cooled from molten rock. Water changed it later.

In short

Under the delta, Jezero’s floor is not lake mud. It is igneous rock, which means rock that formed from cooled molten rock. Perseverance found two layers. The lower one, Séítah, is coarse-grained and rich in olivine, a mineral that crystallises from molten rock. It formed slowly at the bottom of a body of magma. The layer above it, Máaz, is probably lava flows, or the matching part of the same magma body.

What scientists found

  • Thin coatings of iron and magnesium carbonate sit between the mineral grains. This points to water rich in carbon dioxide, in conditions where water was scarce.
  • Two kinds of salt, sulfates and perchlorates, fill tiny holes in the rock. They were probably left behind later, when salty water (brine) dried up near the surface.
  • Rock cores from here are stored on the rover in case they can be returned to Earth.

Why it matters for understanding ancient Mars

These rocks record two stories in one place: how the crater floor first formed from molten rock, and how water changed it afterwards. That gives scientists a timeline, with a volcanic floor first and water arriving later.

Why it could matter for human exploration OUR EXPLANATION

Perchlorates are a safety issue. A 2013 study in the International Journal of Astrobiology said perchlorate in Martian soil could be a critical chemical hazard to astronauts, although it might also be a source of oxygen. Finding perchlorates in Jezero’s rocks helps scientists learn where these salts occur, which is useful for planning how to protect future crews and equipment.

Sources: Farley et al. 2022, Science · USGS record of the study · Davila et al. 2013 · Image page (NASA)

Feature 4 of 4 · Crater rim

The carbonate-rich margin

A band of carbonate-rich rock along the inside of the western crater rim.

In short

Along the inner edge of the western rim there is a narrow band of light-toned, fractured rock. Orbital data show strong signs of carbonate minerals there. Carbonates form when carbon dioxide (CO2) from the air reacts with liquid water. NASA says this unit played a key role in choosing Jezero as the landing site.

What scientists found

  • How these rocks formed was a puzzle. Ideas included a lake shoreline, volcanic ash or flows, or lava. In 2024 NASA still called the origin largely a mystery, partly because more than 3 billion years of change may have hidden the original textures.
  • A 2026 study in Science concluded that the rocks began as olivine-rich igneous rock formed underground. Later, water containing CO2 changed much of it into carbonate. The lake boosted this change at lower levels but was not the only cause. Rocks higher on the rim keep more of their original olivine.
  • Other research teams are still testing other ideas, including shoreline processes, so this story may keep changing.
  • Three samples from the margin unit have been collected for possible return to Earth.

Why it matters for understanding ancient Mars

Carbonates are a record of Mars’ past water and CO2, which are clues to its early atmosphere and climate. On Earth, carbonates can also trap and fossilise microbes, so scientists study them closely for any sign of past life.

Why it could matter for human exploration OUR EXPLANATION

To get from the delta to the upper rim, the rover drove more than 10 km and climbed about 400 m. That shows how much geological history can sit within one crater, which matters when deciding where explorers should spend their limited time and energy.

Sources: NASA Science blog, 2023 · NASA Science blog, 2024 ·

4. Terrain & Hazards

REAL DATA What makes Jezero Crater hard to land on and hard to travel across, based on NASA and JPL reports and photographs. Boxes marked OUR EXPLANATION are our own reasoning about what these hazards could mean for future astronauts. They are not NASA plans.

Please read first. This website has no slope map, rock map or safety rating for walking or driving at Jezero, so we do not give one. The numbers below are facts that NASA published about the rover’s landing, driving and observations. Nothing here means that any place is safe.

Hazard 1 of 5

Slopes and steep terrain

Steep ground and loose surface material can make a rover slip, lean over or get stuck.

What it is

A slope is how steeply the ground tilts. Jezero has steep ground, including cliffs that NASA lists among its landing hazards, and the inner wall of the western crater rim, which Perseverance has climbed.

Why it is dangerous

  • A rover that leans too far could tip over. After running thousands of simulations, NASA’s team raised Perseverance’s tilt limit (how far it may lean while driving) to 24 degrees.
  • Loose ground can matter more than the angle. On the first part of the climb up the crater rim, a hill of 15 to 20 degrees was far less firm than expected. The wheels had very little grip and many drives stopped early.
  • NASASpaceflight, reporting NASA’s update, said the rim surface is loose dust and sand with thin, brittle crusts. On many days the rover covered only about half the distance it would on firm ground, and on one day only about 20% of its planned route.

How it affects a rover

Slipping slows exploration and can make drives stop early. The team tried driving up the slope backwards and zig-zagging across it, because tests on Earth showed the rover’s suspension keeps better grip when driving backwards.

How it could affect future astronauts OUR EXPLANATION

People on foot or in a vehicle would also lose grip on loose slopes, and a fall or a tipped vehicle far from base would be serious. The 24-degree limit is a rule for this particular rover. It does not tell us what is safe for a person or another vehicle. Because we have no slope map for Jezero, this website does not rate any slope as safe.

Sources: NASA Science blog, tilt limit · JPL robotics paper (PDF) · NASASpaceflight, 2024 · NASA/JPL, October 2024 · NASA/JPL hazard map page

Hazard 2 of 5

Rocks and boulders

Large rocks can block the way, and some are too small to see from orbit.

What it is

Rocks of all sizes lie on the ground at Jezero. A boulder field is an area densely covered with large rocks. NASA lists rocks and boulder-strewn areas among the hazards here.

Why it is dangerous

  • A large rock can block the way or trap a wheel. Clearance under the rover matters: NASA’s team reduced the clearance required under the rover’s belly pan to 20 centimetres (about 7.9 inches) after its experience with boulder fields and steep slopes.
  • Orbital photos do not show every rock. In the Snowdrift Peak boulder field, more than half a kilometre wide, the straight-line route was 520 m, but the rover logged 759 m, partly because its self-driving system steered around rocks that were not visible in orbiter images.

How it affects a rover

Boulder fields force slow, careful driving and detours. NASA says Perseverance’s self-driving system, AutoNav, crossed this field in about a third of the time earlier rovers would have needed. That shows how much the rover depends on its own hazard detection.

How it could affect future astronauts OUR EXPLANATION

A boulder field could force people to detour, and every extra kilometre uses time and supplies. Uneven, rocky ground is also a tripping risk. Because orbital pictures miss some rocks, a route that looks clear from above could still hold surprises. We have not modelled this.

Sources: NASA/JPL image page · NASA/JPL release on AutoNav, reposted by Astrobiology.com · NASA Science blog, clearance · NASA/JPL hazard map page

Hazard 3 of 5

Dust and visibility

Wind lifts dust and sand at Jezero. Dust devils are common, and blown sand can damage equipment.

What it is

Dust is very fine powder on the ground. Wind can lift it into the air. A dust devil is a whirlwind loaded with dust.

Why it is dangerous

  • NASA reported that at least four whirlwinds pass Perseverance on a typical Martian day, and more than one per hour during a peak hour just after noon. Jezero may sit near a “dust storm track” that crosses the planet.
  • The largest dust devils seen by the rover’s navigation cameras were about 270 m across. One daytime wind event covered an area about ten times larger (reported by Space.com).
  • The rover’s wind sensors were seriously damaged. NASA’s explanation was that Jezero’s dust lifting was so intense that sand carried in whirlwinds harmed them.

How it affects a rover

Blown sand can wear out exposed parts, as it did the wind sensors. Perseverance is nuclear-powered, so it does not rely on solar panels that dust could cover.

How it could affect future astronauts OUR EXPLANATION

Dust could get onto suits, visors and tools and into airlocks. A 2013 study, “Perchlorate on Mars: a chemical hazard and a resource for humans”, says perchlorate in Martian soil could be a chemical hazard to astronauts. We found no measurements of how much a dust devil or dust cloud would limit an astronaut’s view, so we give no number for visibility.

Sources: NASA/JPL, June 2022 · Space.com report on the same study · NASA/JPL image page · Davila et al. 2013

Hazard 4 of 5

Landing and exploration hazards

The spacecraft had to find a safe spot to touch down in very rough terrain.

What it is

Landing means setting the rover down on the surface. NASA says a large part of the Jezero landing area was covered with hazards, including cliffs, dune fields a rover could not escape from, and rocks.

Why it is dangerous

A NASA engineer said landing on one of these hazards could be catastrophic for the whole mission. Past landers could not have attempted a place this rough. Jezero was wanted for its science but was previously considered too hazardous to land on.

How it affects a rover

Perseverance used Terrain-Relative Navigation. During descent it compared camera images with an onboard map and steered away from hazards. NASA says this cut the error in knowing the lander’s position to about 40 m relative to that map, and that a later Mars sample-return lander would need it too. The system chose a landing spot several kilometres from the delta, so the rover later had to drive about 5 km back to reach it.

How it could affect future astronauts OUR EXPLANATION

A crewed mission would face the same trade-off between a safe landing spot and an interesting science target. The longer the trip between them, the more time, energy and supplies it would use. We have not calculated this for Jezero.

Sources: NASA/JPL hazard map page · NASA technical report on the landing system · NASA, Terrain Relative Navigation · JPL robotics paper (PDF)

Hazard 5 of 5

Where exploration may be easier

Some areas have fewer known hazards. None of them can be called safe from the data we have.

What NASA shows

  • On NASA’s hazard map, blue and green mark safer areas for landing and red marks landing hazards. It was made for landing, not for driving or walking.
  • The rover came down at a spot reported to be tilted only about 1 degree, with rocks small compared with its wheels (as reported by Spaceflight Now from NASA team members).

What this could mean for planning OUR EXPLANATION

If planners had to choose a starting area, they would probably look for gentle slopes, few large rocks, few dunes and cliffs nearby, and a short path to the science targets. The trade-off is real: the safest landing spot was several kilometres from the delta, which was the main place scientists wanted to study.

What we would need to do this properly

We do not have these yet, so we do not rate any area as suitable:

  • A slope map made from elevation data.
  • A map of how many rocks cover the ground.
  • Information on how firm the ground is in different places.
  • A version of the hazard map with real coordinates.

Sources: NASA/JPL hazard map page · Spaceflight Now, landing report · JPL robotics paper (PDF)

5. Perseverance Rover

REAL DATA NASA’s Perseverance rover has been exploring Jezero Crater since 2021. This section explains its mission, four of its instruments, how it looks for signs of ancient life, and what it has found. Boxes marked OUR EXPLANATION are our own reasoning, not NASA plans.

Not a live tracker. This section does not show where the rover is now or the route it has driven. For that, use NASA’s own location map. Findings are described as NASA published them, on the dates given, and science keeps changing.

Mission overview

Perseverance is a NASA rover that landed in Jezero Crater on 18 February 2021. It is designed to collect rock samples that could one day be returned to Earth. NASA’s scientists want to know whether Jezero could have supported microbial life long ago, and whether any such life left evidence behind.

  • Landed: 18 February 2021, in Jezero Crater.
  • Four goals: find out whether life ever existed on Mars, describe its climate, describe its geology, and prepare for human exploration.
  • Seven main instruments: Mastcam-Z, MEDA, MOXIE, PIXL, RIMFAX, SHERLOC and SuperCam.
  • Built and run by: NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL).

Sources: NASA/JPL Mars 2020 press kit, science · Image page (NASA)

Four main instruments

Perseverance carries seven main instruments. Here are four of them. Two sit on the mast, which is the rover’s “head”, and two sit on its robotic arm.

On the mast

SuperCam

What it does

It fires a pulsed laser at a rock or soil target up to about 7 metres (20 feet) away. The laser heats a tiny spot to about 10,000 °C and makes a bright spark. SuperCam studies the glowing gas to learn what the target is made of. A microphone also records the sound of the laser zaps.

Why it matters

It lets scientists test rocks the rover cannot reach and decide where to look more closely. It builds on ChemCam, a similar instrument on the Curiosity rover.

On the mast

Mastcam-Z

What it does

A pair of zoomable cameras on the mast. At full zoom they can see something as small as a house fly from as far away as a soccer field is long.

Why it matters

Its pictures show the geology and colours of the landscape, which helps scientists work out the area’s history. They also help the team plan where to drive and which rocks to study.

On the robotic arm

PIXL

What it does

PIXL aims a tiny but powerful X-ray beam at a rock. The rock gives off a glow that depends on which chemical elements it contains. PIXL can detect more than 20 elements, and it can analyse a spot as small as a grain of sand in about 10 seconds.

Why it matters

By scanning many spots it shows where each chemical sits in a rock. That helps scientists work out how the rock’s textures and structures formed.

On the robotic arm

SHERLOC

What it does

SHERLOC sits on the arm near PIXL. It uses an ultraviolet laser to look for organic molecules and minerals in a rock. It also has a camera called WATSON that takes close-up pictures of rock grains and textures.

Why it matters

Organic molecules and minerals help scientists decide which rocks are worth sampling and how a rock formed.

Sources: NASA/JPL Mars 2020 press kit, science (all four instruments and images)

How Perseverance investigates a rock

The rover does not just scoop up whatever is nearby. For each sample it follows a careful routine, so that scientists know exactly what they are collecting.

  1. Abrade the surfaceAn abrasion tool grinds a circle about 5 cm (2 in) wide into the rock. This removes the dusty, weathered outside and shows fresh rock and its texture.
  2. Look closelyPIXL measures which chemical elements are in the rock. SHERLOC looks for organic molecules and minerals.
  3. Check the targetCameras photograph the rock and its neighbours from many angles to confirm it is the one the team wants.
  4. Cut a coreThe drill cuts out a core of rock about 1 cm (0.4 in) wide.
  5. Check the coreCameras photograph the core in the drill and the rock after coring.
  6. Seal itThe core is moved into a sealed tube and stored inside the rover.

The sealed tubes are meant to keep the samples safe for a possible return to Earth. NASA’s 2021 plan was that Perseverance might leave some tubes as a “cache” at depot sites on the surface, so a later mission could collect selected tubes and bring them home for study. Plans like this can change, so check NASA for the latest.

OUR EXPLANATION Why this order?

Only selected rocks are cored. The first three steps use instruments that do not use up the rock, so they work as a way to decide whether a rock is worth sampling. This is our reading of NASA’s description, not a NASA statement.

Sources: NASA Science blog, 2021 (updated 2024) · Mastcam-Z team, Arizona State University: sample cores

Key discoveries so far

Four findings from Jezero, in the order they were reported. Each one links to a NASA or journal source.

Reported 2021

A lake and a river delta

What was found

Layers in a hill called Kodiak look like the layers of river deltas on Earth. Boulders in the delta slopes were carried in by powerful flash floods.

Why it matters

It confirmed that a river and a lake really existed at Jezero, so the landing site was a place where life could have existed.

Source: NASA/JPL, October 2021

Reported 2022

A volcanic floor changed by water

What was found

The crater floor is igneous rock, formed from cooled molten rock. Water later altered it and left carbonate minerals and salts in its grains and holes.

Why it matters

The same rocks record both how the floor formed and how water changed it afterwards.

Source: Farley et al. 2022, Science

Studied 2023 onwards

Carbonate-rich rocks on the rim

What was found

A band of carbonate-rich rock runs along the inside of the western rim. Its origin was a puzzle. A 2026 study in Science concluded that it began as olivine-rich igneous rock that CO2-bearing water later changed. Other teams are still testing other ideas. Three samples from this unit have been collected.

Why it matters

Carbonates are a record of Mars’ past water and carbon dioxide.

Sources: NASA Science blog, 2024 · Williford et al. 2026, Science

Found 2024, reported 2025

“Leopard spots” on Cheyava Falls

What was found

Perseverance found a reddish rock with spots made of two iron-rich minerals, vivianite and greigite. On Earth these often form in muddy sediments around decaying organic matter, and some microbes make greigite. The rock sits in the Bright Angel formation, and the rover took a core called “Sapphire Canyon” from it. NASA said it was one of 27 rock cores the rover had collected up to September 2025.

This is not proof of life. NASA calls it a “potential biosignature”, meaning something that might have a biological origin but needs more study. The minerals can also form without life, and NASA says it makes the data available so other scientists can confirm or refute the biological potential. Scientists rate confidence in life detection on a seven-level framework called CoLD.

Why it matters

The rocks are among the youngest sedimentary rocks the mission has studied. That hints that Mars may have stayed habitable for longer, or later, than scientists once thought.

Source: NASA/JPL, September 2025

Preparing for people

One of the rover’s four official goals is to prepare for human exploration. Three of its experiments show what that means in practice: making oxygen, measuring the weather and dust, and testing spacesuit materials. All of them are tests done by a robot. None of them proves that people are ready to go.

Making oxygen · MOXIE

Oxygen from the Martian air

What it did

MOXIE (the Mars Oxygen ISRU Experiment) turned carbon dioxide from the Martian atmosphere into oxygen. It ran 16 times and made 122 grams of oxygen in total. Its peak rate was 12 grams an hour, about twice NASA’s original goal, and the oxygen was 98% pure or better. It worked across a full Mars year, in different seasons and conditions. Its last run was on 7 August 2023.

What it does not prove

NASA says MOXIE does not show that the technology is ready for crewed use or for making industrial quantities. A full-scale system would need a bigger oxygen generator, plus a way to liquefy and store the oxygen.

OUR EXPLANATION Why it matters for people

Astronauts need air to breathe, and rockets need large amounts of oxygen as propellant to come home. Making oxygen on Mars instead of carrying it from Earth could save cargo. But 122 grams is a very small amount compared with those needs, so this was a first test, not a solution.

Sources: NASA/JPL, September 2023 · NASA/JPL Mars 2020 press kit

Weather and dust · MEDA

A weather station on Mars

What it does

MEDA measures wind speed and direction, air pressure, humidity, air temperature and sunlight. A small camera called SkyCam measures how dust and ice in the air change the sunlight reaching the ground.

What it has found

In its first 216 Martian days Perseverance recorded hundreds of dust devils. At least four passed on a typical day. Sand carried by whirlwinds damaged both of MEDA’s wind sensors, so the team added a protective coating.

OUR EXPLANATION Why it matters for people

NASA says dust storms threaten robotic and human explorers, and that this knowledge could eventually help scientists predict them. Any crew working outdoors would need that kind of forecast. We do not have data here on how strong dust or wind would need to be to stop outdoor work.

Sources: NASA/JPL Mars 2020 press kit · NASA/JPL, June 2022

Spacesuit materials · SHERLOC target

Testing suit fabric on Mars

What is being tested

The rover carries five small (20 mm) swatches of spacesuit material on a calibration target. They are a polycarbonate helmet visor, Vectran (used for glove palms), two kinds of Teflon, and Ortho-Fabric, a layered fabric containing Nomex, Gore-Tex and Kevlar. They were the first spacesuit materials sent to Mars. The aim is to see how they wear down in Martian conditions.

Early results (March 2025)

About half of the changes SHERLOC saw in the samples happened in the rover’s first 200 Martian days, and Vectran appeared to change first. A science paper was being prepared, and NASA scientists planned ground tests in chambers that simulate Mars. NASA had reported no results from those tests.

Sources: NASA/JPL, March 2025 · Image page (NASA)

Dust on the swatches · PIXL

What sticks to a spacesuit

What was found

NASA reported on 26 October 2026 a study of fine dust on the swatches, covering 1,645 Martian days. It is described as the first on-site measurement of such tiny Martian dust particles. PIXL detected particles smaller than 2.5 micrometres and even ultrafine ones around 100 nanometres. Earlier Mars measurements could only resolve particles of about 3 micrometres.

  • Particles on the Teflon and Vectran swatches averaged about 2 micrometres. On other samples they ran down to roughly 100 to 300 nanometres.
  • Static electricity appears to decide which sizes stick to which materials. The finest particles stayed on through years of driving and drilling.
  • Some materials held less dust than others, which could help choose suit materials and coatings.
  • Wind has rounded the dust grains, so NASA compares them as less abrasive than lunar dust. This was not tested directly on the swatches.
  • The dust is rich in sulfur, chlorine and iron, and has more silica than lunar dust.

This does not measure health effects. The study did not test any effect on people. NASA notes that Martian dust carries perchlorates and reactive iron, and that scientists think the risk depends on how much dust people breathe and for how long. Ideas such as suitports and dual-chamber airlocks to keep dust out of living quarters are possibilities, not tested results.

Source: NASA, 26 October 2026 (study in PNAS)

OUR EXPLANATION What this adds up to

These three experiments each prepare for a different need: resources (oxygen), the environment (weather and dust) and equipment (suit materials). They are small robotic tests, and they leave many questions open. They help NASA decide what to test next, and they do not mean astronauts are ready to go. 6. Route Planner

CALCULATION SIMULATION Choose a start and a destination from places in and around Jezero Crater. The distance is a calculation from their Mars coordinates. The route is a planning exercise only, not an approved NASA route.

This is a straight-line distance. It is the shortest path over the surface of a sphere the size of Mars, ignoring hills, rocks and craters. A real drive would be longer, and nothing here means a route is safe. The coordinates are rounded to 0.01 degrees (about 0.6 km), so distances of a few kilometres or less are rough.

How is the distance calculated?

We turn each place’s latitude and longitude into a position on a sphere, then measure the shortest arc between the two positions (the haversine formula). The sphere has Mars’ radius of 3,396.19 km, the value USGS uses for its Mars maps. Earth’s radius would give the wrong answer, so we do not use it. Mars is not a perfect sphere, so this is a close approximation, not a survey measurement.

      04 Our Methodology

      How JEZERO uses science. Tap each item to expand.

      Map data & imagery
      Scientific source

      The Interactive Jezero Map displays Mars orbital imagery from NASA Mars Trek. We display it; we did not produce or alter the imagery.

      Geographic coordinates
      Scientific source

      Location coordinates come from the map's coordinate system and published geologic maps. Tapping the map reports the coordinates of that point.

      Mars distance calculation
      Our calculation

      The Route Planner computes great-circle (straight-line surface) distance between two coordinate pairs using Mars' radius. Mars' mean radius is a published constant (IAU report, listed above).

      The formula and the arithmetic are ours. The result is a geometric distance, not a measured one.

      Route planning
      Our calculation

      Route Planner results are straight-line distances between two named locations. They do not account for slopes, boulders, dunes, crater walls or traversability.

      EVA simulation assumptions
      Hypothetical

      Oxygen, battery, suit condition, crew size, durations and emergency events are invented, simplified values chosen for teaching. They are not NASA suit specifications.

      Survival simulation assumptions
      Hypothetical

      Scenarios and outcomes in the Survival & Emergency Guide are simplified models. General topics (thin atmosphere, radiation, dust, cold) reflect real Martian conditions, but thresholds and outcomes are illustrative.

      05 Project Limitations

      What this prototype can and cannot do.

      • The Route Planner calculates simplified straight-line distances, not validated or navigable rover paths.
      • The EVA simulator uses hypothetical assumptions, not actual NASA suit or life-support specifications.
      • Survival scenarios are simplified educational models, not certified life-support predictions.
      • This website does not control, command or receive live data from any rover or spacecraft.
      • Map imagery has limited resolution and may not show hazards a real mission would need to assess.
      • Our summaries of scientific findings are simplified; the original publications are the authoritative source.
